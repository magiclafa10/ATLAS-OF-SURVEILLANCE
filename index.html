EFF methodology & limits

The Atlas of Surveillance is built on open-source intelligence, or OSINT. In the words of its authors, OSINT means "gathering information that already exists online—from news stories, social media posts, press releases, or documents buried in government websites, often turned up through using advanced search engine techniques" (EFF, Methodology).

The dataset is a comprehensive archive that documents the adoption of surveillance technologies by thousands of municipal, county and state law enforcement agencies in the USA. Each "case" in the dataset records a specific instance of a technology being purchased, implemented or used by a U.S. government agency.

In an era of digital panopticon, this project aims to reverse the gaze. If the authorities monitor the citizens, OSINT allows citizens to monitor the watchers. This analysis uses the version of the dataset updated on December 4, 2025.

Mission objectives

This investigation aims to dissect the American surveillance apparatus by identifying the private companies driving this expansion. The analysis focuses on three pillars:

Why New Jersey ranks #2

The position of New Jersey is largely an effect of how the data were collected. The EFF imported into the Atlas the 2020 body-worn camera survey of the New Jersey Attorney General, which covered law enforcement agencies across the whole state. New Jersey also has hundreds of small municipal police departments, each counted as a separate agency. Both factors multiply the number of records, regardless of how intensively residents are actually monitored.

Critical analysis 2 documents

[A] The security paradox — a hypothesis

The ranking raises a question rather than proving an answer: documented surveillance does not seem to match the geography of violent crime.

Safe yet recorded: New Jersey (#2) and Massachusetts (#9) are among the states with the lowest violent crime rates (FBI Crime Data Explorer), yet they rank among the states with the most records.

Dangerous yet less recorded: states with historically high violent crime rates per capita (such as Louisiana, New Mexico or Alaska) are absent from the top ten, and Tennessee (#8) ranks below New Jersey despite higher crime.

Hypothesis: adoption may follow institutional capacity—budgets, federal grants, procurement offices and the sheer number of agencies—more than crime levels. As the note on New Jersey shows, part of the effect also comes from how the data were collected. Testing the hypothesis would require normalizing records by population and number of agencies, and comparing them with crime and income data.

[B] Across the political divide

The top of the ranking includes both historically Democratic states (California, Illinois) and historically Republican states (Texas, Florida).

Whether the political justification is police "reform" and accountability, or strict "law & order" enforcement, surveillance technology is adopted on both sides. This is an observation on four large states, not a statistical test, but it suggests that the expansion of police surveillance is not a partisan phenomenon.

The tools

The toolbox Which technologies are adopted?

12 categories

The Atlas classifies every record into one of twelve technology types. The full glossary is in the folder below the chart.

To understand the nature of modern surveillance, one must classify the tools employed. The following data breaks down the most frequently acquired technologies by U.S. law enforcement agencies.

The surveillance ecosystem is composed of 12 primary technological pillars.

Technological database 12 items
01Body-worn cameras
Mobile audio-video recording systems worn on an officer's uniform. Originally intended for police accountability, they also function as mobile surveillance units.
02Automated License Plate Readers (ALPR)
High-speed camera systems, mounted on street poles or police cars, that automatically capture license plates and location data, creating a map of citizen movement.
03Drones (UAVs)
Unmanned aerial vehicles equipped with high-resolution cameras, thermal imaging, and sometimes facial recognition capabilities for aerial monitoring.
04Third-party investigative platforms
Subscriptions to private databases (e.g. data brokers) that aggregate consumer data, social media and public records for law enforcement mining.
05Face recognition
Biometric software that maps facial features from a photo or video and matches them against massive databases (like DMV records or mugshots) to identify individuals.
06Camera registry
Programs (like "Operation Shield") that map the locations of private home and business security cameras, allowing police to quickly request footage.
07Gunshot detection
Networks of acoustic sensors (e.g. ShotSpotter) placed in neighborhoods to detect and triangulate the sound of gunfire, alerting police dispatch instantly.
08Real-Time Crime Center (RTCC)
Centralized command hubs that integrate data streams from cameras, ALPRs and calls for service to provide live intelligence to officers in the field.
09Predictive policing
Algorithmic software that uses historical crime data to forecast where future crimes are likely to occur or who might commit them, directing patrol resources.
10Cell-site simulator
Devices (often called "Stingrays") that mimic cell towers, tricking nearby phones into connecting to them to intercept location data and communications.
11Video analytics
AI-driven software layered over video feeds to automatically detect "anomalies", recognize objects or track specific individuals across multiple cameras.
12Fusion center
State-owned information centers that serve as focal points for sharing threat-related information between federal (FBI/DHS) and local agencies.

Intrusiveness

Not all eyes are equal Which states use the most invasive technology?

Definition

A "dystopian state" is measured by how it watches, not only by how much.

Not all surveillance is created equal. This section analyzes the quality of the technology deployed, rather than just the quantity. We define a "Dystopian State" not merely by how much it watches, but by how it watches.

The chart below visualizes the ratio between states that rely on standard, visible hardware versus those that invest heavily in invisible, invasive or predictive technologies.

High risk / invasive

Technologies that operate covertly or continuously, predict behavior, or aggregate data from many sources, often without public knowledge. These represent the "dystopian" shift in policing.

  • Drones (UAVs)
  • Facial recognition
  • Cell-site simulators (Stingrays)
  • Predictive policing (PredPol)
  • Fusion centers
  • Real-Time Crime Centers (RTCC)
  • Third-party data brokers
  • Gunshot detection
Common use / standard

Tools that have become standard, widely deployed equipment for modern law enforcement, often cited for accountability or basic monitoring. "Standard" does not mean harmless.

  • Body-worn cameras
  • Automated License Plate Readers (ALPR)
  • Camera registry
  • Video analytics
Classification note

The split reflects how established a tool is in everyday policing, not only how much it can reveal. ALPR and video analytics can track people at scale, but they have become so widespread that they are treated here as the new baseline. Gunshot detection is classified as high risk because its acoustic sensors listen continuously in residential neighborhoods. A different choice—for example moving ALPR into the high-risk group—would change the index, especially for states with large plate-reader networks.

Method

For each state, the index compares the records of "high risk" technologies with those of "common use" tools, as shown in Exhibit D.

Case study

Facial recognition: the Michigan case

Context

Detroit's "Project Green Light" links private business cameras directly to police command.

The map above shows why Michigan is a key case for the normalization of biometric surveillance in the United States: face recognition is available to police statewide through the State Police network (SNAP). The risk is that the technology moves beyond catching criminals towards indexing the population.

The "always-on" watch

Beyond the dataset. The Atlas only records law enforcement agencies. The trends below come from wider public reporting on facial recognition in the United States and show where the technology is heading; they are not measured in Exhibit E.

  1. Stadiums as labsSports venues have piloted systems where your face replaces your ticket. Access to public entertainment can come at the price of your biometric data.
  2. Retail surveillanceSome shopping centers have adopted facial recognition to scan customers entering the premises, cross-referencing them against private "watchlists", often without their informed consent.
  3. Indexing the innocentPerhaps most disturbingly, these systems also capture mere passersby. When every face recorded by street-level cameras can be searched, anonymity in public space becomes practically impossible.

Follow the money

Who are the vendors?

Note on names

Vendor names were normalized in OpenRefine (e.g. Fusus grouped with Axon). Counts are approximate.

The landscape of surveillance is not just about the police; it is about the private entities supplying the tools. The bar chart below ranks vendors by the number of records in which they appear. Most records name no vendor; among those that do, a few private companies recur far more often than all the others.

What a record is

One record documents one adoption of one technology by one agency. It says nothing about the value or the duration of the contract, nor whether the technology is still in use: as the EFF itself warns, some programs are abandoned without anyone reporting it. Record counts measure presence, not market share in dollars.

Full vendor analysis 4 groups

Based on the dataset extracted, here is the breakdown of suppliers by magnitude and functional category, distinguishing between private giants and public entities.

1 The dominant group: undisclosed (N.A.)

Over 7,000 cases. This represents the largest share of the dataset. These records indicate technology adoptions where the vendor is not named in the available sources. This can reflect confidentiality clauses, national security reasons or lack of contract transparency (e.g. cell-site simulators in Anchorage or drone usage in smaller municipalities), but also simply that the original news story or document did not mention the supplier.

2 The private market giants

  • Flock Safety (~2,700 cases): primary supplier of Automated License Plate Readers (ALPR) like the Falcon and Condor models. Also produces audio gunshot detection (Raven) and autonomous response drones.
  • Axon / Fusus (~1,100 cases): Axon is the dominant supplier of body-worn cameras and the evidence cloud (Evidence.com). Fusus (now Axon) provides the "Real-Time Crime Center" cloud, unifying public and private camera feeds.
  • SoundThinking (~900 cases): formerly ShotSpotter, leader in acoustic gunshot detection. Also provides predictive policing software (ResourceRouter) and investigative platforms (CrimeTracer).
  • DJI (~700 cases): Chinese market leader for UAVs (drones) used for aerial footage and tactical monitoring.
  • Motorola Solutions (~600 cases): a complete ecosystem including body-cams, drones, ALPR (Vigilant Solutions), and the citizen camera registry platform CityProtect.

3 Specialists & big data brokers

  • State biometrics (Idemia & NEC): Idemia provides algorithms for state databases (e.g. Michigan, Florida), while NEC manages regional facial recognition systems like WideNet.
  • Algorithmic specialists: includes Geolitica (formerly PredPol, predictive policing; it ceased operations at the end of 2023, but its past contracts remain in the records), Dataminr (real-time social media monitoring) and ZenCity (public sentiment analysis).
  • Big data brokers: companies like LexisNexis, Thomson Reuters and TransUnion provide investigative platforms that cross-reference massive amounts of public and private data (e.g. Accurint, CLEAR, TLOxp).
  • Spyware & interception: niche providers like Harris, Octasic and Cellebrite supply cell-site simulators (Stingrays) and phone extraction tools.

4 Public vendors (state systems)

In these cases, a state agency acts as the technology provider for local police:

  • LACRIS: Los Angeles County biometric database serving hundreds of local agencies.
  • FACES: Pinellas County Sheriff's system, granting access to 25 million photos.
  • SNAP: Michigan State Police statewide biometric network.
  • Tennessee Highway Patrol: centralized IBM predictive analytics system shared with 95 sheriffs.

Who supplies what

Vendors presence Who produces what?

Tip

To zoom in and see the origin of less common technologies, move the count slider in Exhibit G from 3949 to 19.

This interactive visualization shows, for each surveillance technology, how its records are distributed among vendors. It allows you to explore which companies appear most often for drones, body cams or facial recognition.

Pay attention

In almost every category, the "Not Available" (black) bar is prevalent. This represents the shadow market: records where the supplier's identity is not reported, whether because it was withheld, redacted, or simply never mentioned in the source.

The "black hole" anomaly

When observing Fusion Centers and Real-Time Crime Centers (RTCC), you will notice they are almost entirely "Undisclosed" (black).

Why is this data hidden?

Unlike a drone or a camera, which are products bought from a catalog, Fusion Centers are operational infrastructures. They are not simply "bought"; they are established through federal grants and partnerships with the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) and the FBI.

The "vendor" in these cases is often a complex web of federal grants, government contracts and defense contractors. Part of their operations falls under national security protocols, which limits standard public procurement transparency. Effectively, the state itself is the vendor.

Consequence for the numbers: because no vendor is named, these state-run infrastructures are counted as "undeclared" (black) in Exhibits H and I, not as "state infrastructure" (blue). The real public share is therefore higher than the 2.10% shown in Chapter 09.

08
Control

Nature of technology Private, public or hidden?

Color key

The same three colors are used in Exhibits H and I.

We have classified the vendors into three categories to map the control of security:

State infrastructure (blue). The "Public Brain". Records in which the vendor is a public body, such as a state or county system (e.g. LACRIS, SNAP). Fusion Centers and Real-Time Crime Centers belong conceptually here, but are counted in blue only when a public vendor is named.
Private corporation (red). The "Profit-Driven Senses". Technologies provided by for-profit companies acting as rented senses for the State (e.g. drones, ALPR, bodycams).
Undeclared vendor (black). The "Transparency Gap". Cases where technology is adopted but the vendor is not cited in public documents. This group mixes undisclosed contracts, public infrastructures without a named supplier, and incomplete sources.
System alert: privatization of force

Critical observation: where a vendor is identified, it is in the vast majority of cases a private company: 52.03% of all records, against 2.10% public.

This points to a deep privatization of security across the United States. The public sector (the police) is becoming structurally dependent on the private sector to function. When public safety relies on proprietary algorithms and corporate hardware, the line between state authority and corporate profit becomes increasingly blurred.

The privatization of security

Reading the grid

The analysis of the Atlas of Surveillance dataset points to a clear pattern: where the supplier is known, police surveillance relies overwhelmingly on private technology.

How to read these numbers

Both the private and the public shares are lower bounds: part of the 45.87% without a declared vendor is supplied by companies, and part is run by the State itself (see the Fusion Center anomaly in Chapter 07).

The most robust figure is the ratio among identified vendors: 52.03 private against 2.10 public means that about 96% of known suppliers are private and about 4% are public.

The nature of security: police as a client

Police have always bought equipment from companies; what changes is the shift from owning tools (cars, paper files) to renting digital services.

Rented senses (SaaS)
The State rents its "eyes and ears". Companies like Axon don't just sell hardware; they manage the entire cloud evidence system.
Black box algorithms
Predictive policing logic (e.g. Geolitica, formerly PredPol) is corporate property, largely inaccessible to public scrutiny or democratic control.
Concentration
A few actors recur in critical segments, such as Flock Safety (license plates) and SoundThinking (gunshot detection).

Systemic & social risks

Delegating surveillance to a private oligopoly creates immediate dangers.

Erosion of sovereignty
Security becomes a commodity. If the State stops paying the subscription, it loses access to its own criminal data.
Algorithmic bias
Private algorithms can disproportionately target minorities without any democratic oversight.
Profit vs. rights
Companies push for invasive tech (like Clearview AI facial recognition) to maximize revenue, ignoring civil liberties.
Projection

Three possible futures if current procurement trends continue.

Future threat scenarios

Scenario 1

State becomes dependent

Police departments become "locked-in" to vendor ecosystems. Public safety budgets are drained by licensing fees, forcing departments to prioritize revenue-generating policing (fines) over safety to pay the software bills.

Scenario 2

Automated segregation

"Black Box" algorithms classify entire neighborhoods as "high risk" based on biased historical data. This creates a feedback loop where residents are constantly monitored and arrested, justifying further spending on private tech.

Scenario 3

Data leakage and sale of private sensitive data

Biometric data collected by police bodycams and drones flows into private servers. Without strict regulation, this sensitive data could be sold to insurance companies, advertisers, or foreign actors.

Conclusion

The privatization of surveillance in the USA is not just a change in procurement; it is a change in governance. Police have always bought equipment from private companies; what is new is that they now rent data infrastructures, software and analytics that the companies own and control. Citizens' safety no longer depends on the state alone, but increasingly on private companies that provide security technologies.

When the public sector (the police) becomes structurally dependent on the private sector (corporations) to function, public safety ceases to be a right and becomes a product.

Citizens risk becoming not only constituents to be protected, but also raw data to be harvested for profit.

Technical challenge: the "shadow data"

The greatest challenge of this study was not visualization, but data cleaning and restructuring. The original dataset presented critical obstacles requiring months of analytical work. Cleaning and standardizing thousands of records from heterogeneous sources was a slow, meticulous process.

The enigma of N.A./NULL data: a significant discovery emerged from a technical obstacle. I encountered massive amounts of missing data (N.A. or NULL) in the vendor column.

My decision: instead of ignoring these voids, I interpreted them as political data. My hypothesis is that "Null" data often marks a transparency "black zone": contracts protected by Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs), trade secrets or national security protocols. Not every void has this meaning (sometimes the source simply did not name the vendor), but together these gaps became an integral part of the thesis, covering 45.87% of the records.

Limits of the data

Like every OSINT archive, the Atlas of Surveillance has blind spots. They do not invalidate the findings, but they shape how they should be read:

Coverage
Visibility biasThe Atlas is built from news stories, public records and official documents. Agencies that publish more, or attract more media attention, are more visible than others.
Scale
Absolute countsRecords are not normalized by population or by number of agencies, so large states rank higher almost by default.
Collection
Statewide importsSome states were covered by complete official surveys imported into the Atlas (e.g. New Jersey's 2020 body-camera survey), which inflates their counts compared with states documented only through news.
Unit
Records, not contractsA record is one documented adoption. It carries no information on contract value, duration or whether the technology is still in use.
Gaps
Missing vendorsAn empty vendor field can signal secrecy, but also an incomplete source or a state-run infrastructure. The 45.87% is therefore an upper bound of the "shadow market", not a precise measure.
Choices
Author's classificationThe split between "high risk" and "common use" tools, and the three vendor categories, are analytical choices of this study, not categories of the original dataset. Other reasonable choices (e.g. ALPR as high risk) would change the Dystopian Index.
Time
A snapshotThe analysis reflects the dataset as of December 4, 2025 (the EFF has updated it since). A single snapshot shows a pattern, not a historical trend.

Next steps

To turn the hypotheses of this study into tested findings: normalize records by population and number of agencies; compare them with crime and income data; reclassify Fusion Centers and RTCCs as public infrastructure and recompute the shares; and repeat the analysis on the latest version of the dataset.
